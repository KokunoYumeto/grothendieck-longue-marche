                                                  homology equivalence from its geometric realisation to (the analytification of) the moduli stack of

                                          1. Introduction
Background and aim. Let Mg,n denote the moduli stack of stable genus g nodal curves with
n marked points, also know as the Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compactification (when n = 0, we
omit the n). In 1984, Charney and Lee defined a category SCg , called the category of stable curves,
and proved that there is a rational homology equivalence from the geometric realisation |SCg | to
(the analytification of) the coarse moduli space of Mg ([CL84]). In 2008, Ebert and Giansiracusa
provided an integral refinement of this theorem: generalising the category SCg to include marked
points, they both generalised and strengthened the result by showing that, in fact, the geometric
realisation of the resulting category SCg,n has the same homotopy type as the underlying analytic
stack Man                                     an
          g,n . Moreover, both SCg,n and Mg,n come equipped with natural stratifications and the
established homotopy equivalence is functorial with respect to inclusions of strata and closures of
strata ([EG08]).
The purpose of this paper is to further strengthen these results: we prove that the category SCg,n
captures not merely the homotopy type of the stack but the stratified homotopy type. The fact that
SCg,n is a 1-category may be the interpreted as saying that Mg,n is a K(π, 1) in a stratified sense. The
most important feature of the stratified homotopy type is the resulting classification of constructible
sheaves, that is, sheaves which are locally constant along each stratum. As a consequence, we obtain
an identification of the category of constructible sheaves on Mg,n with the presheaf category on
SCg,n ; moreover, we can identify the derived constructible category with a derived functor category.
To the best of the author’s knowledge, such a classification is new and it may provide a useful tool
for studying cohomology of Mg,n with coefficients which are not necessarily constant nor even locally
constant.
Our result cements the fact that the intuition displayed by Charney–Lee in 1984 goes far beyond the
established rational homology equivalence (as is also evident when reading their paper); their under-
standing of the Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compactification precedes the development of stratified
homotopy theory ([Tre09, Lur17]) and the present paper should be viewed as a mere footnote to this
story. We simply establish what was anticipated by the methods used in [CL84].
Stratified homotopy theory. Let us give a brief overview of stratified homotopy theory to provide
some intuition of the invariant in question. It is a classical result in algebraic topology that there is
an equivalence between locally constant sheaves on a sufficiently nice topological space and repre-
sentations of its fundamental groupoid, the so-called monodromy equivalence. In unpublished work,
MacPherson made the observation that analogously to how locally constant sheaves are classified by
paths in the topological space, the constructible sheaves on a stratified space can be classified by
so-called exit paths. Constructible sheaves are sheaves which are locally constant along each stratum,
and exit paths are stratum preserving paths, that is, an exit path can move into “more open” strata,
but not the other way. Treumann gave a 2-categorical version of this result ([Tre09]), and Lurie has
developed the ∞-categorical setting ([Lur17, Appendix A]). To a sufficiently nice stratified space,
Lurie associates an exit path ∞-category; this is a refinement of the fundamental ∞-groupoid incor-
porating the additional structure of a stratification. Roughly put, the exit path ∞-category captures
the homotopy types of the individual strata and moreover the “homotopy type of the glueing data”,
           MODULI STACK OF STABLE CURVES FROM A STRATIFIED HOMOTOPY VIEWPOINT                           3

that is, how these individual homotopy types fit together. Stratified homotopy theory has been
used and developed in several directions in recent years: model structures and a stratified homotopy
hypothesis ([AFR19, AFT17, Dou21, Hai21, NL19]); exodromy for schemes ([BGH20, BH19]); and
concrete calculations ([Cep19, Jan22]).
In [Jan23], we provide a generalisation of stratified homotopy types to stratified topological stacks:
in the spirit of [CJ21], we opt for a non-constructive approach and define the exit path ∞-category
of a stratified topological stack as the idempotent complete ∞-category classifying constructible

Outline of paper. In Sections 2 through 4, we introduce and recall the necessary objects, con-
structions and properties enabling our final calculation. Let us stress that these sections contain
no original material; we simply present the relevant background in a way that fits into the setting
at hand. We do provide an alternative description of the category originally defined by Charney
and Lee ([CL84], cf. Construction 3.18), but this is a simple observation albeit absolutely essential
to our calculational strategy. We do not pretend to provide a complete review of the background
and refer instead to other sources for details and proofs. Our motivation (or excuse) for giving a
detailed review is that, to the best of the author’s knowledge, the moduli stack of stable curves has
not previously been studied in the setting of stratified topological stacks; therefore we choose to
introduce quite a lot of details in order to provide rigorous setup.
In Section 2, we introduce the moduli stacks in question: the Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compact-
ifications and their underlying stratified topological stacks. In Section 3, we introduce the category
of stable curves, and in Section 4, we recall Teichmüller space and its Harvey bordification and make
some preliminary observations.
The original material is presented in Sections 5 and 6 and follows the calculational strategy of [CJ21].
In Section 5, we exploit various descent properties of sheaf categories to exhibit the inductive nature
of the Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compactifications and, more importantly perhaps, we provide a
diagrammatic analogue of these inductive decompositions. The actual calculation is done in Section
6 where we combine the established inductive decompositions with certain permanence properties of
exit path ∞-categories. The idea is to compatibly cover Mg,P by stratified spaces whose exit path
∞-categories identify with their stratifying posets — this should be interpreted as the analogue for
stratified spaces of being contractible. We exploit the so-called Harvey bordification to construct
this cover. The inductive nature of Mg,P and the diagrammatic analogues allow us to show that
sheaves on Mg,P are determined by compatible sheaves on the cover (Theorem 6.4). In the end, this
allows us to calculate the exit path ∞-category as a colimit of posets in Cat∞ .
We include a small appendix on dual graphs for the reader unfamiliar with these details. We also
include a rather comprehensive appendix identifying the Harvey compactification as constructed by
Ivanov ([Iva89]) with the real oriented blow-up of the Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compactification
— this is an observation originally due to Looijenga ([Loo95]).

                    2. The Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compactification
In this section, we introduce the moduli stacks of interest, namely the moduli stack of stable nodal
curves, also known as the Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compactification. We start by introducing
the algebraic stacks and go on to consider their underlying topological stacks and their natural
stratifications in terms of stable dual graphs. We refer to [ACG11] for details.

2.1. Stable curves and a range of moduli stacks. What follows is a speedy recap of the objects
under consideration; we refer to [ACG11, Chapter XII, §8] for details on (1-)stacks.
By a curve we mean a reduced, proper, connected scheme X of dimension 1 over C. By the genus
g of a curve X we mean its arithmetic genus g = dimC H 1 (X, OX ). A node (or an ordinary double
point) of X is a point p ∈ X(C) with the property that the completed local ring O    cX,p is isomorphic
to C[[x, y]]/(xy) as a complete C-algebra. A curve is nodal if its only singularities, if any, are nodes.
Let P be a finite set. A P -pointed nodal curve is the data (X; {pi }i∈P ) of a nodal curve X and a
collection of distinct smooth points pi ∈ X, i ∈ P . We often write (X; pi ) leaving P implicit.
A P -pointed nodal curve (X; pi ) is said to be stable if its group of automorphisms is finite, where
the automorphisms are required to fix the marked points pointwise. We will from now on assume
that all curves considered are nodal and thus refer to stable nodal curves simply as stable curves;
we say that such a curve is smooth if we want to stress that it has no singularities. It is well-known
that there exist stable P -pointed genus g curves if and only if 2g − 2 + |P | > 0 (see e.g. [ACG11,
Chapter X, Lemma 3.1]); from now on, whenever consider a pair (g, P ), we will implicitly assume
that 2g − 2 + |P | > 0.
Remark 2.1. A bijection P ∼    = P ′ of finite sets turns a P -pointed curve into a P ′ -pointed curve, so
we can always reduce to P = {1, . . . , n} for some n. It is useful, however, to be able to mark by a
general finite set, for example when it comes to the so-called clutching morphisms (see e.g. [ACG11,
Chapter X, §7]). We will not need to go into the finer details of these morphisms, but they do play
an important role in our calculational strategy, so we choose to work in this generality.                ◦

               3. Stable nodal surfaces and associated objects of interest
In this section, we introduce what we will call the Charney–Lee categories; this is a variant of the
category of stable curves introduced in [CL84]. We want to work with a different and more algebraic
incarnation of this category that fits better into the setting of stratified homotopy theory. In order
to define these, we introduce stable nodal surfaces, their mapping class groups and the poset of
admissible curve systems. We also provide an alternative description of the stratification poset of
stable dual graphs.

3.1. Stable nodal surfaces and their mapping class groups. We recall the definition of stable
nodal surfaces of finite type and their mapping class groups. By a surface we mean an oriented
smooth 2-manifold.
Let P a finite set. By a P -pointed surface of finite type, we mean a compact connected surface
S equipped with an embedding P ,→ S. We refer to the image of P as the marked points and
denote these by pi , i ∈ P , or simply by P ⊆ S. We denote the P -pointed surface of finite type by
(S, P ) leaving the embedding implicit. The homeomorphism type of the punctured surface S ∖ P is
determined by the topological genus g of S and the number |P | of marked points ([FM12, §1.1.1]). A
P -pointed surface of finite type (S, P ) is stable if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions:
    (1) the Euler characteristic of the complement of the marked points is negative, χ(S ∖ P ) < 0;
    (2) 2g + 2 − n > 0 where g is the genus of S and n = |P |;
    (3) S ∖ P admits a hyperbolic metric ([FM12, Theorem 1.2]).
12                                      MIKALA ØRSNES JANSEN

In order to make a clean inductive argument for our final calculation it will be beneficial for us to
work more generally with stable nodal surfaces. This is purely a technicality and a nodal surface
is. as one would expect, just the result of glueing a finite collection of stable surfaces together along
pairs of marked points, thus introducing nodes.
Definition 3.1. By a topological nodal surface of finite type, we mean a topological space S such that
every point has a either a fundamental system of neighbourhoods homeomorphic to the unit disk in
R2 or a fundamental system of neighbourhoods homeomorphic to the double cone in R3 , i.e. the result
of glueing two unit disks together at the origin. The points with the latter type of neighbourhoods
are called nodes. For a finite set P , a P -pointed topological nodal surface of finite type is a compact
connected topological nodal surface S together with an embedding P ,→ S ∖ {nodes}.                      ◁
The following construction is just a topological model for normalisation of the nodes.

3.2. Curve posets. We recall admissible curve systems, introduce the curve poset and compare it
with the poset of dual graphs.
By an admissible curve system on a stable nodal surface (S, P ), we mean a collection C of disjoint
simple closed curves in S∖({nodes}∪P ) such that no single curve bounds a disk nor a once-punctured
disk, and no two curves bound a cylinder.
14                                       MIKALA ØRSNES JANSEN

Definition 3.10. Let (S, P ) be a stable nodal surface. We define the curve poset of (S, P ) to be the
poset CS = C(S,P ) of isotopy classes of admissible curve systems on (S, P ) with partial order given
by reverse inclusion: σ ≤ τ if and only if the isotopy classes of disjoint simple closed curves in τ are
a subset of those in σ. We include the empty curve system ∅ as an element in CS .                      ◁
Remark 3.11. The realisation of (CS ∖ ∅)op is the classical curve complex associated to (S, P ),
that is, the simplicial complex with vertices the isotopy classes of simple closed curves not bounding
a disk nor a once-punctured disk, and where distinct vertices c0 , . . . , cn span an n-simplex if the
representing curves can be homotoped to be pairwise disjoint. The curve poset CS is then the
opposite of what is sometimes called the augmented curve complex (e.g. [CL21]). We choose to work
with reverse inclusion as our partial order because this better aligns with the setting of stratified
homotopy theory. Comparing with the situation of the reductive Borel–Serre compactification, the
classical curve complex corresponds to the Tits building, whereas the curve poset corresponds to the
poset of parabolic subgroups.                                                                        ◦

Remark 3.16. We could exhibit the stratification of the coarse moduli space using the poset ΓS \CS .
Indeed, the moduli space is homeomorphic to the quotient of the so-called augmented Teichmüller

4.1. Teichmüller space. We review the Teichmüller space TS = T(S,P ) associated to a stable nodal

We recall first of all the definition of a stable Riemann surface with nodes. By a Riemann surface
with nodes, we mean a compact, connected, complex analytic space such that every point has a
neighbourhood isomorphic either to the disk in C or to the cone {xy = 0} in C2 ; the latter points
are called nodes.

Let us very briefly recall the idea behind the Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates (see [FM12, §10.6] for

     (2) a choice of orientation of each curve ci in C and for each pair of pants a choice of three arcs
         connecting the three boundary components (or punctures).

4.2. The bordification: construction, stratification and quotient. As mentioned, the Harvey

In this appendix, we introduce the notion of a stable dual graph which enters into the story via the
natural stratification of the Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compactification.
By a graph, we mean a finite connected (multi-)graph, that is, we allow multiple edges and we will
also allow half-edges. More precisely, a graph G = (V, E, ι) consists of the following data:
       • a set of vertices V ;  `
       • a set of half-edges E = v∈V Ev equipped with a partition indexed by V ;
       • an involution ι : E → E.
The pairs of half-edges interchanged by ι are called edges, the set of half-edges fixed by ι are called
legs, and a half-edge e ∈ E is said to be incident to v if it belongs to Ev . We will generally omit ι
from the notation.

Definition A.1. A dual graph is a pair (G, ω) where:
      (i) G = (V, E) is a graph as above;
     (ii) the weight function ω : V → Z≥0 , v 7→ gv , is any function on the set of vertices.
The genus of a dual graph (G, ω) is
                                                              X

                                                             v∈V


For a finite set P , a P -pointed dual graph is a triple (G, ω, m) where (G, ω) is a dual graph as above
and the P -marking m : P → E is a bijective labelling of the legs of the graph.
For any vertex v ∈ V , we write nv = |Ev |, that is, the number of half-edges which are incident to v.

        Appendix B. The Harvey compactification as a real oriented blow-up
In this appendix, we identify the Harvey compactification of Mg,P as constructed by Ivanov [Iva89]
with the real oriented blow-up of the Deligne–Mumford–Knudsen compactification of Mg,P . Exhibit-
ing the Harvey compactification as a real oriented blow-up is originally due to Looijenga [Loo95] and
the details are essentially given in [ACG11, Chapter XV§8]. In both of these sources, however, the
Harvey bordification plays an auxiliary role as the bordification of interest is instead the so-called
augmented Teichmüller space. For this reason, we choose to make the explicit identification here,
taking Ivanov’s model as our starting point. We also take care to identify the stacks and not just of
the underlying coarse moduli spaces. We mostly follow [ACG11]; more specifically §9 of Chapter X
on real oriented blow-ups and §8 of Chapter 15 on the boundary of Teichmüller space.
We refer to [ACG11, pp.149-151] for a recap of real oriented blow-ups as needed in our situation (see
also [Gil11] for the general case and basic properties). We refrain from explicitly recollecting the
construction here as we will in any case be referring to [ACG11] for many of the finer details in this
appendix. Given a smooth analytic space X with a normal crossings divisor D ⊆ X, we denote the
real oriented blow-up of X at D by BlD X, or if D is implicitly given (as for example the boundary),
then we may write Bl∂ X. For us it suffices to deal explicitly with real oriented blow-ups of polydisks
B with D a collection of coordinate hyperplanes (as spelt out in [ACG11]). Since the real oriented
blow-up is a locally defined construction, we can generalise to Deligne–Mumford stacks.
