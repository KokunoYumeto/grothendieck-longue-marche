                                              Abstract
           We compute equations, integral Groebner bases, fibres and point groups for the final
       modular group scheme in sections 48–50 of La Longue Marche. It is flat of relative
       dimension six and smooth exactly away from two. We prove pinning and parametrization
       results, correct the integral sheaf presentation, compute geometric central extensions and
       the vanishing manuscript Brauer class, and construct exponential counterexamples and the
       virtually abelian envelope. Every statement carries a proof status. Remaining comparison,
       extension and universality questions have proved cases and executed computations. No
       priority is claimed.

A.2. Exactly when a regular unipotent lift is unique                [Proved]
    Let R be a ring and let U ∈ GL2 (R) have trace 2, determinant 1 and a non-scalar image in
every residue field. The matrices with those properties and the same image as U in PGL2 (R)
are exactly
                           (1 + τ )U,    τ ∈ R, 2τ = 0, τ 2 = 0.
Here “same image” is equality of sections of the fppf quotient; the scalar relating two global
matrix lifts is global. Consequently uniqueness on R-points is equivalent to {τ ∈ R : 2τ =
τ 2 = 0} = 0. For a scheme S the analogous statement uses Γ(S, OS ); uniqueness after every
base change is equivalent to 2 being invertible on S.

Proof. If U 0 = λU , determinants give λ2 = 1 and traces give 2λ = 2. With τ = λ − 1
these equations are precisely 2τ = τ 2 = 0. Conversely these equations preserve trace and
determinant, and Cayley–Hamilton gives (U 0 − I)2 = 0. They preserve regularity because
λ = 1 in every residue field. The scalar is global since the kernel sheaf of GL2 → PGL2 is Gm .
If 2 is invertible the assertion persists under all base changes. If a characteristic-2 point exists,
base change to k[δ]/(δ 2 ) supplies the nonzero τ = δ.
    The ambiguity is represented by the nonflat finite group scheme

                    H = Spec Z[τ ]/(2τ, τ 2 ),     ∆τ = τ ⊗ 1 + 1 ⊗ τ + τ ⊗ τ.

This is exactly the manuscript’s µ02 , not a finite flat replacement for it (C3:358–360, fols.480–481).
Over Z/4, the two matrices ( 11 01 ) and ( 33 03 ) are a counterexample to unrestricted uniqueness.
Both rings of cardinality 2 and 3 are fields, so cardinality 4 is minimal. The computation
checks all stated equalities, not only their reductions modulo 2.

GL2 (R) conjugacy class. Their SL2 (R) conjugacy classes are naturally indexed by R× /(R× )2 .
In particular there are respectively 2, 2, 4, 2 such classes over Z, Z/4, Z/8, Z/9; for a field the

Proof. A.3 gives transitivity. The common centralizer of r, s− is the scalar group: commuting
with their sum diag(0, 1) forces a matrix to be diagonal, and commuting with s− makes

determinants differ by the determinant of a scalar, namely a square. Every unit is the
determinant of diag(a, 1). This proves both completeness and separation of the invariant.

A.8. Unrestricted uniqueness among all group-scheme isomorphisms                       [Refuted]
    The modular data cannot determine a unique arbitrary group-scheme isomorphism GL2 →
GL2 , even over Z or Q. The correct uniqueness in A.3 is among algebra-induced inner
identifications. Indeed
                                    θ(g) = (det g)−1 g
is a nonidentity involutive automorphism of GL2 fixing SL2 pointwise, hence fixing r, s and
both root subgroups.

Proof. Determinants are central scalars, so the formula is multiplicative. In rank two det θ(g) =
(det g)−1 , and hence θ2 (g) = g. On the centre it sends aI to a−1 I; an inner or algebra-

The program verifies multiplicativity and involutivity universally. Thus a uniqueness claim
encompassing all group-scheme isomorphisms needs the inner/algebra condition, independently
of the characteristic-2 infinitesimal ambiguity.

A.10. The low-type convention table [Known]
    Let Sg,n be an oriented closed surface of genus g with a set of n distinct marked points
removed. Write Π = π1 (Sg,n ), Mod+ for orientation-preserving mapping classes allowing
permutations of those points, PMod+ for the subgroup fixing them individually, and Mod±
for the full group allowing orientation reversal. All quotients are by isotopies through homeo-
morphisms preserving the marked set. The points are punctures, not boundary circles fixed
pointwise. With C2 = Z/2, the complete table in the six types specified in Rule 5 is

                            (g, n)     Π    Mod+        PMod+     Mod±
                            (0, 0)     1    1           1         C2
                            (0, 1)     1    1           1         C2
                            (0, 2)     Z    C2          1         C2 × C2
                            (1, 0)     Z2   SL2 (Z)     SL2 (Z)   GL2 (Z)
                            (0, 3)     F2   S3          1         S3 × C2

Their centres, keeping the surface group and the two full mapping-class groups separate, are

The pure oriented centres are trivial in the sphere rows and {±I} in the torus rows. The

Proof. Van Kampen gives Fn−1 for a sphere with n > 0 punctures, Z2 for the closed torus,
and F2 for the once-punctured torus. These fundamental-group calculations are independent
of a choice of marked-point labels. The mapping-class computations are verified in Farb and
Margalit (2012, §§2.2.1–2.2.2, Proposition 2.3, Theorem 2.5, the following once-punctured-torus
paragraph, and §8.1); the cited PDF is version 5.0, pp. 51–57 and 230–231.
    Here is the elementary reduction, including the convention-sensitive parts. On a sphere
with three fixed points, an arc joining two points in the complement of the third can be
isotoped to its image, with endpoints fixed. After fixing the arc pointwise, cutting along it
leaves a disc with one marked point. The Alexander isotopy, with this point at the centre, fixes
both the point and the boundary. Thus the pure oriented group is trivial. For fewer marked
points, extra points can first be fixed by isotopy in their complement; the same argument
applies. Every permutation of at most three points is realized by an orientation-preserving

                                                   10
homeomorphism. A reflection of the sphere fixes three chosen points individually. Consequently
the homomorphism recording permutation and orientation is an isomorphism onto Sn × C2
for 0 ≤ n ≤ 3, with S0 = S1 = 1. This proves the sphere rows and the assertions about pure
groups.
       On the torus, action on homology identifies the oriented group with SL2 (Z) by the cited
Theorem 2.5; all integral invertible matrices are realized by linear maps, and orientation is
their determinant. Forgetting one marked point is an isomorphism on mapping-class groups.
To check injectivity without an erroneous point-pushing kernel, put that point at 0. If f fixes
0 and Ft is an isotopy from f to the identity on the unmarked torus, then x 7→ Ft (x) − Ft (0)
is an isotopy fixing 0. Surjectivity follows from the linear representatives, which already fix 0.
       A reduced-word calculation gives CF2 (a) = hai and CF2 (b) = hbi, so Z(F2 ) = 1. The other
surface-group centres follow from commutativity. The centre of S3 is trivial, since commuting
with both (12) and (23) forces a permutation to be the identity. A matrix commuting with
( 10 11 ) and ( 11 01 ) is scalar; an invertible scalar integral matrix is ±I. This computes the centres
of both torus groups and proves the table.

Scope of the comparisons. The first four types have 2g − 2 + n ≤ 0. Their geometric
groups are trivial or abelian, and their profinite completions are respectively 1, 1, Z, b Zb 2 , with
those same groups as centres. Hence arguments requiring a centre-free hyperbolic geometric
kernel do not apply; no hyperbolic anabelian reconstruction theorem is being asserted for these
rows. In particular the forgetful torus map above has no injected Z2 point-pushing kernel.
The types (0, 3) and (1, 1) are hyperbolic. Their abstract groups are both F2 = ha, bi, but
their peripheral classes are respectively a, b, (ab)−1 and [a, b]−1 . Their distinct peripheral data
and mapping-class centres must therefore be retained in a comparison.
     Even for the cylinder, orientation and loop action differ: if  is orientation sign and s
permutation sign, the induced sign on Π = Z is s. Indeed z 7→ 1/z exchanges the two
punctures and preserves orientation, whereas z 7→ z fixes them and reverses orientation; both
act by −1 on Π. This also explains why isotopy cannot be replaced here by arbitrary homotopy.
     These are discrete topological groups, not the affine group-scheme automorphism functors
in §§48–50. For example the oriented mapping-class group of the sphere is trivial while its
complex algebraic automorphism group is PGL2 (C). Orientation-reversing mapping classes
still belong to an orientable surface; they do not supply a non-orientable version of the complex-
curve comparison. A real structure adds a specified antiholomorphic involution and its outer
action, not just a choice of the abstract extended group. The supplied LM3 A.11 was used as
a lead; the calculation and the cited primary exposition were checked independently here.

Proof. Use the tree whose vertices are homothety classes of Zp -lattices in Q2p , with adjacent

determinant-one group preserves the parity of that difference and has no edge inversions.

E.6    The covering test and the two rational theories

E.21. The Stallings lead, with the precise completions              [Proved]
    Let F  = hx, yi be the discrete free group, use [x, y] = xyx−1 y −1 , and define f (x) = x,
f (y) = y[x, y]. For every finite c, f induces an automorphism of F/γc+1 F . It induces continu-
ous automorphisms of every pro-p completion and of the rational prounipotent completion.
Nevertheless the action x 7→ (12), y 7→ (13) on three letters is transitive, while its pullback
along f has orbits {1, 2}, {3}.

Proof. The induced map on F ab is the identity. The group γi F/γi+1 F is generated by iterated
commutators of weight i in x, y, and replacing either generator by itself times a commutator
changes such an expression only in weight at least i + 1. Thus f is the identity on each
successive quotient. Induction in the central extensions

                          1 → γc F/γc+1 F → F/γc+1 F → F/γc F → 1

proves that it is an automorphism at every stage. The inverses are compatible; passing to
rational unipotent versions of these nilpotent quotients and then taking limits gives the asserted



                                               79
prounipotent automorphism. This proves the needed special case of the Stallings conclusion
directly.
    For a pro-p group generated by x, y, their images under f generate its Frattini quotient,
since they agree there with x, y. They topologically generate the group: otherwise their closed
subgroup would be contained in an open subgroup of index p, contradicting that assertion.
Thus the induced endomorphism is surjective. A finitely generated profinite group is Hopfian.
Indeed it has only finitely many open normal subgroups of any fixed index; inverse image
under a surjective endomorphism is an injective self-map of each such finite set and hence a
permutation. Its kernel must therefore lie in every open normal subgroup, and is trivial. This
proves the pro-p assertion for every prime.
    For a = (12), b = (13), direct permutation multiplication gives b[a, b] = a. Hence the
pulled-back action has both generators acting by (12). The program computes the two orbit
decompositions exactly. This also shows why neither the individual pro-p quotients nor the
inverse system of nilpotent quotients can recover this particular finite action: S3 is not
nilpotent. No assertion is made here that every discrete feature of F is lost, or that this was
Grothendieck’s historical reason for introducing §50.

E.22. The modular envelope detects the three-letter covering                  [Proved]
   The quotient S → S3 induced by SL2 (Z) → SL2 (F2 ) extends to both SM and ES as a
morphism to the constant finite Z-group scheme (S3 )Z . Consequently these envelopes retain
the associated three-sheeted covering. The unipotent completion of S does not.

Proof. Both λ0 and λ1 are the identity modulo 2, so the reduction map kills Π0 and factors
through H = S/M . Its image is all of SL2 (F2 ) ≃ S3 : the reductions of ρ and σ have orders 3
and 2 and generate the six matrices. Compose the component projection of the finite-stage
model, or the proconstant projection of the envelope, with this quotient. The three-letter
permutation representation therefore factors through either scheme. E.18 shows that the
unipotent completion of S is trivial, so this nontrivial action cannot factor through it.
    For the free group in E.21, its free metabelian quotient in the modern sense also retains the
action because S003 = 1. This does not identify that free metabelian quotient with an object
to which §50 applies: it is not virtually abelian. Indeed it surjects onto the two-generated
metabelian group Z o Z. In that group, if an abelian finite-index subgroup existed, it would
contain a nonzero power of the shift and a nonzero power of the lamp at the origin; these do
not commute, since the shift moves that lamp to a different position. A quotient of a virtually
abelian group is virtually abelian, giving the contradiction. The manuscript’s terminology and
the reviewer’s modern use of “metabelian” have different scopes.

E.23. What the two rational theories retain on this example                  [Known]
   For the pointed classifying space BS, fibrewise rationalization retains the discrete funda-
mental group S and has no higher homotopy groups. Toën’s schematization has fundamental
group PQ (S). In this example it is BPQ (S). The envelope of §50 is instead the integral
group scheme ES , whose rational representations are exactly the quasi-unipotent rational
representations of S. The full pro-algebraic completion is strictly larger, even for this particular
group.

Proof. Rivera et al. (2021, Theorem 3; repeated as Theorem 16) identify fibrewise rational
equivalences by the unchanged discrete π1 and the rationalized higher homotopy groups.
Applying that statement to the Eilenberg–Mac Lane space BS, whose higher homotopy groups
vanish, gives the first assertion with no nilpotence assumption. The same conclusion follows



                                                80
directly from its loop algebra Q[S] and its category of all derived local systems; here arbitrary,
not only finite-dimensional, modules are allowed.
    Toën (2006, Corollary 3.3.7) identify the fundamental group of schematization with the full
pro-algebraic completion. For completeness we check that no higher schematic homotopy is
needed for this example. A finitely generated free abelian group M is algebraically good over
Q: on each finite-dimensional representation, its group cohomology is computed by the Koszul
complex of the commuting operators gi − 1. After extending scalars to an algebraic closure,
decompose into simultaneous generalized characters. If some character value is not 1, the
corresponding operator is invertible and contracts the complex. On the unipotent block, gi − 1
differs from log gi by a commuting invertible factor, so the complex is the Lie-algebra cohomology
complex of the additive vector group. These are exactly the cohomology complexes for the pro-
                                                                                 ∗
algebraic completion of M . Explicitly, over Q this completion is Gra × D((Q )r ): simultaneous
multiplicative Jordan decomposition gives the diagonalizable factor, and logarithms of the
commuting unipotent parts give the additive factor. Its diagonalizable factor has exact
invariants, by the direct grading decomposition, so precisely the trivial-character block remains
in cohomology. Every finite collection of algebraic characters descends to a number field and
then, by restriction of scalars, to a rational representation; hence this description applies to
the base change of the rational completion. Equality of the cohomology comparisons descends
by faithful scalar extension.
    The finite extension 1 → M → S → H → 1 gives the corresponding extension of pro-
algebraic groups. One can construct it by the pushout of the cocycle of E.12 along M → PQ (M );
density proves its universal property for all representations. In both the discrete and algebraic
Hochschild–Serre spectral sequences, taking H-invariants is exact because |H| = 12 is invertible
in Q. The cohomology comparison for M therefore gives the comparison for S, for every finite
dimensional local system. The criterion of Toën (2006, Lemma 3.3.2 and Definition 3.4.1) now
gives (BS ⊗ Q)sch ≃ BPQ (S).
    Finally, induce the character M → Q∗ , λ0 7→ 2, λ1 7→ 1, from the index-12 subgroup M
to S. This gives a 12-dimensional rational representation. The eigenvalues of λ0 include 2,
so it is not quasi-unipotent and cannot factor through the envelope by E.14. The matrices,
the defining group relations, and all twelve eigenvalues are computed in E_envelope.py. This
proves strictness on the requested example, rather than importing the unrelated counterexample
used in the earlier GALOIS package.
    None of these comparisons says that the envelope kills a nontrivial element of S. That
group is residually finite: an element outside M is detected in H, and a nonzero element of M
is detected in some M/nM . Thus its canonical map to the envelope, and consequently to the
full pro-algebraic completion, is injective on the discrete group. What differs is the universal
representation problem and the resulting target, not an elementwise kernel on this particular
input.
