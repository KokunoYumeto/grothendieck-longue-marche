\statement{C.42}{Proved}{One dyadic homology step determines the canonical head}
Let $F$ be a finitely generated free profinite group, let $\sigma$
be an involution, and suppose every involution of
$E=F\rtimes_\sigma C_2$ has centralizer exactly its order-two
subgroup. If $U\trianglelefteq F$ is open and $\sigma$-stable, then
\[
 C_{F/\Phi_2(U)}(\sigma)\longrightarrow F/U
                    \quad\hbox{has trivial image}.              \tag{C.42.1}
\]
More precisely, two actual sections of $U\rtimes_\sigma C_2$
that are conjugate modulo $\Phi_2(U)$ are already $U$-conjugate.
The hypotheses hold for every pure negative sphere action in
C.28, in particular for every full negative pants involution.

For the actual rank-two canonical equation $u=f\alpha(u)$,
all solutions of its induced equation in $F/\Phi_2(U)$ have the
same image in $F/U$, namely the image of the unique full word $u$.
Thus that head value is determined by finite data at this explicit
relative level. This does not compute an unspecified existential
parameter $f$, prove generation, or settle the third cusp.

\begin{proof}
Put $J=U\rtimes_\sigma C_2$, $k=\mathbb F_2$, and let
$t\in H^1(J,k)$ be its quotient character. Set
$M=H^1(U,k)$. The split Hochschild--Serre spectral sequence has
only the two fibre rows. Every invariant character $U\to k$
extends to $J$ by assigning value zero to its specified section;
hence the transgression from quotient degree zero vanishes.
For $N=1+\sigma$ one has
\[
 H^0(C_2,M)=\ker N,\qquad
 H^p(C_2,M)=\ker N/\operatorname{im}N\quad(p\ge1).
\]
Multiplication by $t^p$ maps the invariant row onto every
positive quotient degree. Multiplicativity therefore kills all
remaining differentials. The filtered map
\[
 t^{n-1}:H^1(J,k)\longrightarrow H^n(J,k),\qquad n\ge2,
                                                                    \tag{C.42.2}
\]
is onto both associated-graded pieces, and hence onto. All these
cohomology groups are finite.

The full profinite restriction theorem of
\citet[Theorem 2 and Section 1.1]{CEKochloukovaZalesskii2007}
applies to the open subgroup $U$ of $2$-cohomological dimension
one. No $C_2\times C_2$ embeds in $J$, because every finite
subgroup injects into the quotient $C_2$. Every involution
normalizer is exactly $C_2$ by the hypothesis inherited from $E$.
In degrees above one the theorem gives a dense restriction
embedding into the product indexed by section classes. Its source
is finite, hence its image is closed; consequently this is a
finite product isomorphism. The character $t$ restricts to the
nonzero character on every section. Combining this fact with
(C.42.2) shows that restrictions of $H^1(J,k)$ separate all
section classes.

This degree-one separation is a known theorem of Scheiderer,
explicitly recorded in
\citet[Theorem 2.11 and Remark 2.12]{A_PalQuickFormal2025}; the
preceding split spectral-sequence argument verifies its precise
application here. The explicit finite consequence below is the
additional conclusion being used.

Every character of $J$ kills $\Phi_2(U)$. Thus sections conjugate
modulo this subgroup have identical character values, and hence
belong to the same actual $J$-class. An odd conjugator can be
multiplied by the source section to make its quotient label even,
so conjugacy is already by $U$.

Now lift a $\sigma$-fixed element of $F/\Phi_2(U)$ to $w\in F$.
The sections $\sigma$ and $w^{-1}\sigma w$ lie in $J$ and have
the same image modulo $\Phi_2(U)$. They are therefore conjugate
by some $v\in U$. The equality
$w^{-1}\sigma w=v^{-1}\sigma v$ gives
$wv^{-1}\in C_F(\sigma)=1$. Hence $w\in U$, proving (C.42.1).

Finally, if $w_1,w_2$ solve the finite canonical equation, then
\[
              \alpha(w_1^{-1}w_2)=w_1^{-1}w_2.
\]
Equation (C.42.1) makes their head values equal. The actual full
solution supplies a finite solution, so the common value is its
head. For a finite quotient not initially stable under $\alpha$,
replace its open normal kernel by its intersection with its
$\alpha$-image before applying the statement.
\end{proof}
