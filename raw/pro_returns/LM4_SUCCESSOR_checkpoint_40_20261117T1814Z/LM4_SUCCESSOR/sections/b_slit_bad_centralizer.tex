\emph{B.110.3. The discrete base centralizer.}
The graph is bipartite, with parts
\[
 \{a,b,d,e,p,\bar r\},\qquad
 \{\bar a,\bar b,\bar d,\bar e,\bar p,r\}.
\]
We recall the needed reduced-word fact with its proof. Give every
vertex a stack. Reading a signed letter places its sign on its own
stack and a zero on every noncommuting stack; an opposite terminal
sign cancels together with the matching terminal zeros. Those zeros
are present: an intervening noncommuting letter would have covered
the candidate sign. The operations for commuting letters commute,
and opposite operations are inverse. Conversely each such cancellation
is realized by commuting the inverse letter past exactly the intervening
commuting letters. Successively removing a bottom sign with its matching
zeros recovers a reduced dependence word. Any two available bottom
letters commute, since otherwise one has a zero below its sign.
Thus this is a faithful normal form, and the possible first signed
letters of an element commute pairwise.

If $g\ne1$ is fixed by bar, any possible first $v^{\pm1}$ is
accompanied by $\bar v^{\pm1}$. They must commute, so their orbit
is one of the four diagonal edges, not the $p$ or $r$ pair. Remove
the commuting product $(v\bar v)^{\pm1}$. The remainder is again
fixed and has length two less. Induction shows
\[
 \Gamma^{\langle c\rangle}
   =L=\langle a\bar a,b\bar b,d\bar d,e\bar e\rangle.
\]
Deleting all vertices other than $a,b,d,e$ maps $\Gamma$ onto the
free group on those four vertices and sends the displayed products
to its free basis. They therefore freely generate $L$ and give an
actual retraction onto it. An element $gc^j$ commutes with $c$
exactly when $\bar g=g$, proving $C_B(c)=L\times\langle c\rangle$.

fixes only the base vertex. Hence $C_R(c)=C_B(c)$.
