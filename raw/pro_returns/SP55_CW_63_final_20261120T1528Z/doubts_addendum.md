## Legibility and witness comparison

The principal typescript text is generally readable. The French sheets have worn or doubled machine impressions and small pencil interventions; the English sheets combine typing, handwritten mathematics, proofreading signs and cancellations. The weakest evidence is in pale marginal traces, overwritten old letters and digits, tiny detached punctuation and headings partly hidden by tabs. These weaknesses are local. They do not justify replacing a readable but mathematically unexpected formula with a familiar one.

There are 54 remaining doubt entries. A row can cover repeated instances of the same locally unresolved notation, as with the two subscripts on scan 46. The least secure groups are the detached marginal character on scan 21; faint traces on scans 24, 30, 41 and 44; covered headings on scans 43 and 50; cancelled layers on scans 43 and 46–50; the crowded GF and isolated high marks on scan 51; and the replacement vowel on scan 52. The first candidate is the preferred visible reading, not a silent correction to what the sentence or mathematics would require.

Some doubts concern the intent of a mark whose visible shape can be drawn. The traces on scan 24, the comma and separate point on scan 29, the flourish on scan 41, the two points on scan 47 and the two added shapes on scan 51 are reproduced graphically. Their doubtful status is explained in the table; a further invented letter or illegibility token would duplicate or misrepresent the visible object. Covered words and uncertain readable letters retain their in-place uncertainty or illegibility macros.

Witness C was substantially complete on these particular sheets. This pass records 14 differences against it: one capitalization, one alphabet choice, punctuation and preferred readings in overwritten matter, two small omitted marks, and several changes in how nonverbal marks or crossing strokes are represented. Its many uncertainty marks were useful places to inspect, but the rest of every page was also read. The 85 entries in the certainty section include resolved readings, confirmations of cancellation state and explanations of the scope of a graphical doubt; they are not 85 additional text changes. The small number of C changes is the result of these images and is not adjusted to match rates reported for other batches.

H supplies source text on scans 21, 22, 27, 30, 32, 33 and 34. On these pages it often transcribes annotations while describing substantial typescript passages only in editorial prose. The 89 H-collation rows therefore include 70 omissions, commonly an entire display or paragraph counted as one place. They must not be read as 89 misread words or compared directly with a word-error rate. An H editorial synopsis is not a source transcription. For the other 19 scans, H's descriptions generate no collation rows. Agreement between C and H has never been used as a substitute for visible ink.

All 26 pages were read in order by the same primary reader, first from the whole page and both full-resolution halves, with one crop for each place still open, then once more against the whole image after all first readings were complete. The changes log records the 84 crop boxes, including crops that confirmed a reading without changing it. No OCR, external text, published edition or TeX compilation was used. Mechanical assistance checked file structure, record consistency and explicit finite computations; it did not read the scans or settle a transcription.

The cross-page comparison preserves real differences rather than imposing a uniform alphabet: ordinary and doubled impressions in the French typescript; the different uses of pi, tau, lambda and c in the two texts; and the English manuscript's changes from equality to congruence, including handwritten strokes added to a typed equals sign. The notes distinguish source statements, limited checks, explicit counterexamples and claims carried as background.
