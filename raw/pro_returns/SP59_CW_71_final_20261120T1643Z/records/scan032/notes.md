N1 (targeted scope check). The first assertion holds for distinct underlying simple closed polygonal curves. A proper subset of a circle cannot itself be a circle: remove a point of the larger circle outside the subset, identify the remaining arc with an interval, and note that a compact connected nondegenerate subset is a closed interval, not a circle (removing an interior point disconnects the former but removing one point does not disconnect the latter). Finite edge-pair intersections are segments or points, so their union has finitely many components; as a proper subset of either curve these components are arcs or isolated points. Distinct combinatorial subdivisions with the same underlying curve would be an exception to the word distinct: a triangle and that triangle with one side subdivided have the same underlying set. This qualification is not inserted into the transcription.

N2 (independent re-proof of the hull-boundary assertion and marginal count). Let K=E(Gamma), Gamma prime=partial K and A=Gamma cap Gamma prime. Every hull vertex belongs to A. An endpoint of a nondegenerate common arc must be a Gamma vertex: if it lay inside a Gamma edge, a supporting functional for K would attain its maximum at an interior point of that edge, forcing the whole edge into the supporting line and preventing a contact endpoint there. The same local argument applies to an isolated contact. Each open complementary arc of A on Gamma prime contains no hull vertex and therefore lies in one straight hull side; it is a segment ]s,t[ with distinct endpoints in Phi. Its points miss Gamma and can be joined outward to infinity along a supporting exterior ray, so are in Ext Gamma. At a nonisolated endpoint there is exactly one adjoining gap; at an isolated contact there are two. The neighboring endpoints in those two gaps are distinct because A contains at least the three noncollinear hull vertices. This proves the intended existence/count statement for a nonconvex simple polygon, without altering the corrupted prose or replacing the final C in the corollary.
